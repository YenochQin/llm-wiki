## Problem

How do the chemical and isotopic inventories of Solar System materials constrain the formation pathway of rocky planets, and what does that imply for volatile delivery, primordial atmospheres, and habitability in exoplanetary systems? The review uses meteorites, asteroid sample returns, nucleosynthetic isotope anomalies, volatile depletion patterns, and early Solar System chronology to ask whether terrestrial planets are better explained by slow collisional growth or by rapid growth through pebble accretion within the protoplanetary disk lifetime.

## Key idea

The core synthesis is that planetesimal formation by streaming instability, followed by rapid accretion of drifting pebbles, can reproduce many Solar System chemical and isotopic observables. In this picture, rocky planets do not receive life-relevant volatiles mainly through a late stochastic veneer; instead, water, carbon, nitrogen, and other volatile carriers can be accreted during the main growth phase, with thermal processing during pebble accretion setting the final volatile budget and atmospheric redox state.

## Research classification

- **Theory**: synthesizes streaming instability, pebble accretion, nucleosynthetic fingerprinting, volatile depletion, magma-ocean outgassing, and rocky-planet habitability frameworks.
- **Computation**: uses or reviews thermal modelling of asteroid accretion ages, isotope mixing constraints between NC/CC reservoirs, volatile partitioning models, and population synthesis of rocky planet growth and volatile inventories.
- **Experiment**: synthesizes meteorite isotope measurements, radiometric chronometry, sample-return constraints from primitive asteroids, laboratory geochemistry, and early JWST atmospheric/inner-disk observations.
- **Research objects**: Solar System solids, meteorites, chondrites, differentiated asteroids, Earth, Mars, rocky exoplanets, primordial atmospheres, and protoplanetary disk material.

## Method

This is a review and synthesis rather than a new measurement paper. It organizes evidence from:

- meteorite chronology, including Pb-Pb, Al-Mg, and Hf-W constraints on CAIs, chondrules, differentiated asteroids, chondritic parent bodies, Mars, and Earth;
- nucleosynthetic fingerprinting of NC and CC reservoirs using isotope systems such as Ti, Cr, Mo, Si, Zn, and Zr;
- volatile and moderately volatile element depletion patterns relative to CI chondrites and solar composition;
- models of streaming instability, pebble accretion, thermal processing, radial dust flux, and magma-ocean outgassing;
- small-exoplanet constraints from mass-radius measurements, host-star refractory abundances, JWST atmosphere observations, and population synthesis.

## Results

Major synthesized findings:

- **Early body formation is strongly bimodal.** Differentiated asteroids accreted very early, roughly within the first million years after CAI formation, while chondrite parent bodies accreted later after enough `26Al` had decayed to avoid global melting. This timing naturally fits two phases of streaming-instability planetesimal formation.
- **The NC-CC isotope structure is a formation constraint, not just a taxonomy.** Inner Solar System bodies and outer carbonaceous bodies preserve distinct nucleosynthetic fingerprints, but the review argues that a compositional gradient plus radial transport can better explain some data than a perfectly isolated dichotomy.
- **Pebble accretion can jointly address growth timescales and composition.** Rapid accretion of drifting pebbles can build Mars-like protoplanets within disk lifetimes and can mix inner and outer disk material in proportions inferred from isotope systems.
- **Volatile delivery moves earlier in time.** Under pebble accretion, volatiles and organic species can arrive during main rocky-planet growth, while thermal processing, vapor recycling, and core-mantle-atmosphere partitioning determine how much survives.
- **Atmospheric composition is tied to bulk inventory and mass.** Magma-ocean outgassing and self-oxidation can shift small rocky planets from reducing to oxidizing primordial atmospheres; XUV loss then removes or reshapes part of that inventory.
- **The exoplanet bridge is real but underconstrained.** Super-Earth mass-radius data and host-star abundance proxies suggest broad refractory similarity between rocky planets and their stars, but interior degeneracy and sparse atmospheric detections still limit individual planet interpretation.

## Limitations

- The Solar System evidence is extremely detailed but may not be typical; extrapolating from one system to the exoplanet population remains risky.
- Pebble accretion and collisional growth are not mutually exclusive, and current cosmochemical tracers may preserve time-integrated mixtures rather than a clean mechanism label.
- Several isotope systems are modified by secondary processes such as volatile loss, core formation, and giant impacts, so they cannot always be read as direct source tracers.
- The fate of sublimated volatiles during pebble accretion still needs more advanced hydrodynamic simulations.
- Current small-exoplanet mass-radius and atmosphere data are too degenerate to infer detailed rocky compositions at the level cosmochemistry can reach for Solar System samples.

## Open questions

- How much outer-disk material was transported into the terrestrial planet accretion region during the disk lifetime?
- Does the NC-CC structure represent an early dichotomy, a radial gradient, or a gradient later sharpened by selective transport and accretion?
- Which nucleosynthetic tracers remain robust after volatility-driven processing and core-mantle equilibration?
- Can Zn, Ge, Si, and chondrule rim/core isotope measurements quantify radial dust flux across disk reservoirs?
- How do JWST inner-disk volatile observations connect to radial drift, pebble accretion, and eventual rocky-planet atmosphere composition?
- For exoplanets, when can mass-radius plus host-star abundances distinguish dry, water-rich, carbon-rich, and atmosphere-stripped rocky worlds?

## My take

This paper is a useful bridge between two wiki regions that often stay separate: high-precision Solar System cosmochemistry and exoplanet composition inference. Its strongest wiki role is to make [[pebble-accretion-cosmochemistry]] the mechanism-level counterpart to host-star abundance pages: instead of asking only whether planets resemble their stars, it asks how disk transport, thermal processing, and accretion timing rewrite the starting inventory before a rocky planet becomes observable.

I would treat the headline claim as important but not settled. The review makes a strong case that pebble accretion explains many observables in one coherent frame, but the decisive tests now live in finer isotope surveys, volatile hydrodynamics, and JWST-era volatile maps of inner disks.
